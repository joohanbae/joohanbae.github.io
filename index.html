Research

I work on human–AI interaction for people without domain expertise — the general users who have no way to check whether an AI is helping them or just agreeing with them. My goal is to make AI legible enough that using it well does not require already knowing the answer.

Three threads run through the work.

Telling personalization apart from sycophancy

When a model adapts to you, you cannot tell whether it fitted itself to your situation or simply to your opinion. I treat that indistinguishability as the core problem, and approach it by giving people a view of their own background knowledge and usage habits rather than by explaining the model.

Extracting implicit premises

An LLM response is an enthymeme: an argument with its warrant left out. I develop methods to recover those warrants — from the conversation log together with the user's own background — and represent them as objects a person can accept, reject, or rewrite.

Making the cost of vagueness visible

When an instruction is imprecise, someone pays for it, usually the person, in rework they did not anticipate. TokenTangram meters that cost visually at the moment the command is written.

TokenTangram

First author · concept, system, paper · with Daigo Misaki

A tangram microworld that bills every command at its measured tokens, so you can see who paid for the vagueness — you or the model.

Ask an AI vaguely and it still answers, by filling in what you left unsaid. You never see that work, and no one is billed for it. TokenTangram prices it: a user bar and an AI bar fill side by side as you work.

You can rephrase the command yourself or hand the rest to the model. The counts are measured; the accounting is ours. The meter prices a sentence by a stated rule rather than measuring ambiguity, and it is a probe, not a validated instrument.

Meaning negotiation with generative AI

Master's thesis · in progress · Interaction Support for Meaning Negotiation in Human–Generative AI Collaboration

Making visible how a person and a generative AI line up intent and interpretation, so the person can understand and correct the output themselves.

I define an implicit assumption as whatever is unstated yet necessary for an AI result, and operationalize it as measurable traces in the interaction itself. TokenTangram is the first probe built on that definition.

The motivation is over-reliance: before anyone can calibrate trust in a model, they need a view of what they themselves already know and how they tend to use it. Next is a 12-participant within-subjects study comparing length-based and attempt-based cost displays, planned for December 2026.

Argument as an interaction technique

System design · prototype

A system where the person argues against the AI rather than the other way round.

The model resists, updates its stance with stated reasons, and leaves behind a map of the assumptions that survived the exchange.

Built on Walton's argumentation schemes and Toulmin's warrant-gap framework, with the model's stance governed by explicit condition–trigger rules and critical questions bound to curated per-scheme banks, rather than free generation.

IkigAI

Japan–Germany joint PBL with the University of Münster · circuits, firmware, behaviour, coordination

A conversational robot against loneliness, for older adults. Fifteen people, two countries, five nationalities, one teddy bear that listens.

Japan and Germany are ageing at the same time, and as people live alone and care staff run short, the hours anyone gets to sit with one person are disappearing. Eleven students in Münster, four of us in Tokyo, working through Stanford’s design thinking cycle. The Japanese team took everything except the frame; I ran the English-language coordination between the two sides.

We asked 23 people what makes them feel someone is actually listening. Seventy percent said facial expression — the eyes, as one woman in her seventies put it. Another warned that an expression can also be frightening. So IkigAI is 250mm of 3D print under fur, with circular displays for eyes, servos in the ears that move while it listens, and a microphone in the nose.

The hard part was hardware. Display trouble on the M5Stack CoreS3 traced back to SPI signal integrity and moved the platform to a Raspberry Pi 5 mid-project. Then the amplifier kept dying: the servos were dumping back-EMF into the rail every time the robot moved its head, so it was breaking its own voice each time it tried to express something.

After Münster I brought it back and presented it in Japan, at the 12th Medicine–Pharmacy–Engineering Three-University Symposium — the first time it faced clinicians and pharmacists instead of engineers, who can say what a loneliness scale would have to show before any of this counts.

Poster, October 2026. With Nana Sakai, Mayu Ikeda, Genki Sakai, Daigo Misaki and Victor Taratukhin (Münster / Kogakuin).

We then used the same robot in a 10-participant human–robot interaction study on how people form and revise beliefs about what an LLM-enabled robot can do. That paper is currently under review.

Converging through speech or through AI-refined text

First author · study design, analysis, manuscript · with Victor Taratukhin and Daigo Misaki

Four teams, one design-thinking seminar: what changes when a group converges on an idea by talking versus by passing AI-refined text?

Sixteen engineering students in four teams worked through a 4.5-hour hands-on design-thinking workshop. Some teams converged on an idea through spoken discussion, others through text refined by an AI.

I analyzed convergence ratings, transcripts, and the AI chat logs as four team cases. The journal manuscript is under revision.

Idea Clay

Second author · with Yuya Aikawa and Daigo Misaki

A direct manipulation interface for LLMs built on the metaphor of clay play: merge ideas, tear them apart, never type a prompt.

Ideas and elements sit on a canvas as fragments the user merges, tears apart, prunes, or swaps — five operations that invoke the model without ever asking for a written command.

The design follows Finke's Geneplore model, cycling between generating preinventive structures and exploring them, with Schumpeter's new combination underneath. A study with 12 participants found the metaphor made generation and exploration more intuitive than typing commands, which breaks the momentum divergent thinking depends on.
